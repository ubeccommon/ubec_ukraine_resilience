Seen from Kyiv, Kharkiv or Odesa, the four Carpathian oblasts — Zakarpattia, Ivano-Frankivsk, Lviv and Chernivtsi — are often called "the rear": fewer strikes, fewer alarms, a place where people and businesses go. The data bear this out in part. Over the last 12 months a typical hromada here spent about 108 hours under air-raid alert, against about 690 nationally. Fewer than one in ten of the region's 251 hromadas has recorded even one strike since 2022; nationally it is more than one in five.

Look closer, and "the rear" breaks apart into four quite different places. Night-time light, local budgets and a national survey each tell a different part of the story, and the parts do not always agree.

## 1. The rear that isn't one

Lviv oblast is the fiscally strongest of the four. Its median hromada sits above the national middle on an index of fiscal capacity (own revenue per person, dependence on transfers, share of spending on investment, growth of civilian income-tax receipts). It depends least on transfers from the state. It is also the most exposed: about 15 % of its hromadas have recorded a strike.

The other three are fiscally weaker than the national middle. In Ivano-Frankivsk and Zakarpattia a typical hromada raises only about 5,800 hryvnias per person of its own revenue, against about 9,300 nationally, and relies on transfers for more than half its income. Chernivtsi is weaker still: under 5,000 hryvnias per person, with transfers making up more than half of revenue.

So the rear is not rich. Most of it is quieter than the rest of the country, but poorer in the means a local government can mobilise on its own.

## 2. What the lights show

Satellites measure how much light a place gives off at night. Compared month by month with the same months before the war, this gives a rough index: 1 means as bright as in 2020–21, 0.5 means half.

Nationally, the picture is stark. In March 2022 the median hromada fell to 5 % of its pre-war light — blackout orders and curfews rather than destruction. Light came back through 2023 to about 40 %. It collapsed again during the rolling outages of summer 2024, to about 10 %. Since then it has stayed at around 35–40 %, with no clear upward trend.

The four Carpathian oblasts follow four different curves.

- **Zakarpattia hardly went dark.** Its median hromada was brighter in March 2022 than before the war. In the worst months of 2024 it kept about half its light, and over the last year it has been at roughly its pre-war level.
- **Lviv** sits in between: about 60 % of pre-war light recently, with deep but shorter dips.
- **Chernivtsi** is a little darker, and in more than a third of its hromadas the light has fallen further since 2023 — the national rate.
- **Ivano-Frankivsk** is as dark as the national median, and in the first war winter darker: around 2–4 % of pre-war light in March and November 2022. This is the oblast with the fewest strikes of the four.

These differences are large and consistent. Yet in July 2024 all four fell together — even Zakarpattia dropped to about half its usual light — which points to a cause shared across the grid rather than anything local. What drives the differences the rest of the time is not something this data can show. How each part of the western grid is connected and supplied, and the decisions oblast and hromada administrations have taken about street lighting, are the obvious places to look. I have not verified them. What the numbers do say is that darkness here is not a measure of attack.

## 3. Money that moved west — and partly left again

Local budgets show another movement. Income tax is booked where people are employed. When firms and workers relocated westward in 2022, the Carpathian hromadas' tax receipts rose faster than elsewhere.

In the third quarter of 2022, a typical hromada in all four oblasts collected 13–29 % more civilian income tax, relative to its own 2021 level, than the typical hromada nationally. Ivano-Frankivsk gained most. By mid-2024 the advantage had shrunk to a few percent. In 2025 it came back strongly in Lviv and Ivano-Frankivsk (about 20 % above the national middle), less so in Zakarpattia and Chernivtsi (about 6–14 %).

The 2022 gain went to strong and weak hromadas alike. It had nothing to do with their fiscal capacity. It followed people, not institutions.

The people themselves are counted in two ways, and the difference matters. Registered internally displaced persons in the four oblasts range from 77 to 98 per 1,000 pre-war residents. IOM's survey estimate of those actually living there is lower and strikingly even: 56–60 per 1,000 in all four. Registrations have fallen by 13–18 % since early 2023. Many who registered in the west have since moved on — home, elsewhere in Ukraine, or abroad.

## 4. Trust where light is scarce

The most surprising contrast comes from the SCORE survey (SeeD and UNDP, 2024), which asks people how much they trust local institutions, how cohesive their community feels and how secure they are. Its results are by oblast, not by hromada.

Zakarpattia, the brightest of the four, reports one of the lowest levels of trust in town and village administration in the country: 2.3 on a 10-point scale, more than two points below the national average of 4.5.

Ivano-Frankivsk, among the darkest, reports far higher trust in local administration: 6.3, the highest of the four. It also scores highest in the country on community cohesion and satisfaction with the place people live, and above average on economic security.

Lights, budgets and trust measure different things, and here they point in different directions. A place can keep its lights on and distrust those who run it. A place can go dark and hold together.

## 5. Where capacity matters — and where it doesn't

Across Ukraine, and within the Carpathians, one pattern holds up. During the outage summer of 2024, hromadas with more fiscal capacity kept more of their light relative to their own level of the year before. The link survives using capacity measured in 2021, before the war. What it does not show is why — backup power, better-maintained networks, or simply a different local economy.

The pattern is also partly regional. Ivano-Frankivsk and Chernivtsi together hold 27 of the 33 hromadas in the group I call "weak and hit": low capacity, large outage loss. Lviv holds most of the "strong and steady" ones. Much of what looks like a local story is the grid of the oblast.

Verkhovyna is a small example of the difficulty. Its capacity is low: own revenue about half the national middle, two-thirds of its income from transfers, little investment. It has recorded no strikes. Its tax receipts have run 20–24 % above the national middle since 2022, a lasting relocation gain. Its night-light record rests on fewer lit pixels than the 30 needed to quote a single hromada, so no light value is given for it here.

## What this data cannot tell

- Night light is not recovery. It reflects curfews, street-lighting policy and grid schedules as much as damage or economic life.
- Tax is booked at the employer's address, not where people live.
- The survey is by oblast and excludes occupied areas. Registered and present IDPs differ widely.
- Population figures are pre-war estimates.
- All associations here are between places, at one point in time. None is a cause.

What the numbers do suggest is modest and, I think, useful. The Carpathian rear is not one place, and resilience is not one thing. Money, light and trust move separately. Any recovery programme that treats the region as a single safe hinterland — or measures resilience by one of these alone — will miss most of what is going on.
